People

A small, interdisciplinary research network.

The lab brings together computational, clinical, and neuroscience perspectives. The goal is to train researchers who can move comfortably between a scientific question, a model, and the setting where that model might actually be used.

Lab lead
Mohammad Amin Kamaleddin, PhD
Research Scientist, University of Toronto · Postdoctoral Fellow, St. Michael’s Hospital
Google ScholarORCIDGitHub

Amin’s research develops computational and AI methods across scales—from neural mechanisms and neuromodulation to psychiatric care and population mental health. His current work centers on clinically responsible agentic AI, foundation-model evaluation, causal and interpretable machine learning, and computational neurotechnology.

His training spans biomedical engineering, computational neuroscience, clinical mental-health research, and industry-scale AI leadership. That mix shapes the lab’s central habit: treat methods as tools in service of a scientific or clinical question, not as the question itself.

Training culture

Methods with ownership.

Trainees are expected to understand the assumptions behind the analysis, not only run code. Good projects produce a result, a clear explanation, and an artifact someone else can inspect or reuse.

01Own a defined research question.
02Make analysis reproducible and auditable.
03Communicate the result clearly to technical and non-technical audiences.
Who fits

Curiosity across boundaries.

Strong fits may come from computer science, engineering, neuroscience, psychology, epidemiology, medicine, or adjacent fields. The common requirement is comfort learning outside a single disciplinary box.

01Interest in mental-health questions, not just AI benchmarks.
02Willingness to question model behavior and assumptions.
03Interest in building tools, studies, or analyses others can use.
